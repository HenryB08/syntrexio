# Account Health Scorecard

> **Internal. Never shown to the client.**
> Source of truth: SYNTREX_FOUNDATION.md, Sections 8, 13, and 14.

---

## What this is for

Churn is rarely a surprise to anyone who was watching. It is a surprise to people who were watching the wrong thing.

The wrong thing is revenue, because revenue is unchanged right up until the day it is not. The right things are the signals below, most of which appear weeks or months before a cancellation and almost all of which are recoverable when caught early.

**The three reasons clients actually leave**, in order: lack of proactive strategic guidance, poor communication, and inability to demonstrate value. Price ranks sixth. Every signal in this document is a leading indicator of one of the top three. When you are tempted to explain a churn as a budget decision, check this list first, because it usually is not.

**Targets:** annual churn below 20%. Top performers hold 8 to 10%. Above 30% means stop selling and fix delivery. Net revenue retention above 100%, trending toward 120%.

---

## How to run it

**Scored monthly,** at the same time the Receipt goes out, which is also when you have all the data in front of you anyway. **Reviewed in full at the QBR.**

**Scoring.** Each signal is green, amber, or red.

| | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Green** | Healthy. No action beyond the standard cadence |
| **Amber** | A named action, with an owner and a date, inside 7 days |
| **Red** | A named action inside 48 hours, and the account is on the weekly review list until it is not red |

**Account status is not an average.** It is:

| Status | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Healthy** | No reds, no more than two ambers |
| **Watch** | Three or more ambers, or one red |
| **At risk** | Two or more reds, or any red open longer than 14 days |
| **Critical** | Any red in the first three signals below, at any time |

**Never let a red sit for a full month.** The whole value of a leading indicator is the lead time, and a red logged and left is just a churn you predicted and did nothing about.

# The signals

## Tier one: the three that predict churn earliest

These outrank everything else on the page. A red in any of them makes the account Critical regardless of how the rest scores.

### 1. Intake speed (first 10 days only, then it becomes a permanent account attribute)

A client who completes intake within 48 hours retains above 85%. One who drags past ten days retains below 50%. Onboarding speed predicts retention better than early results do, which means this is scored in the first two weeks of an engagement and its verdict follows the account for its whole life.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Green** | Intake complete inside 48 hours |
| **Amber** | 48 hours to 5 days |
| **Red** | Beyond 10 days |

**What to do**

- *Amber:* call, do not message. Offer a 20 minute screen share and fill it in live while they talk. Half of intake friction is that nobody wants to hunt for a password.
- *Red:* escalate to the economic buyer. Say plainly that the day 7 date is at risk and exactly what it depends on. Then narrow the blocking set to the three items that actually gate the deliverable and get only those.
- *Permanently:* an account that took over 10 days to onboard is a structurally higher-risk account for its whole life. Over-communicate on it for the first two quarters. Do not treat the slow start as water under the bridge.

### 2. The guaranteed metric trend

| | |
|---|---|
| **Green** | On or ahead of the pace needed to hit target inside the window |
| **Amber** | Behind pace with more than 30 days left in the window |
| **Red** | Behind pace with under 30 days left, or the window closed without the target being hit |

**What to do**

- *Amber:* change the approach now, not at the window close. Put the change in the next Receipt in Section 6 rather than waiting to be asked about it.
- *Red:* lead with it. Send the revised plan within 5 business days of the window closing, with new dates, and state in writing that work continues at no additional cost until the metric is hit. A missed guarantee handled loudly retains better than a hit guarantee reported quietly, because the client learns what happens when things go wrong.
- *Never:* renegotiate the baseline or swap the metric for an easier one. That converts a delivery problem into a credibility problem, which is the one you cannot recover from.

### 3. Receipt and cadence discipline (this one is ours, not theirs)

The single most common cause of preventable churn is us, being late.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Green** | Receipt on the agreed day, monthly call held, QBR held on schedule, every month |
| **Amber** | One Receipt late, or one monthly call skipped |
| **Red** | Two Receipts late in a quarter, a QBR skipped or pushed twice, or any month with no Receipt at all |

**What to do**

- *Amber:* fix the calendar, not the intention. Receipts generate on schedule or they do not generate.
- *Red:* this is a delivery failure on our side and it gets treated as one. Acknowledge it to the client in writing, name the date it will be corrected by, and correct it. Then fix the process that let it happen, because it will happen to the next account too.

## Tier two: engagement signals

### 4. Response and approval latency from the client

| | |
|---|---|
| **Green** | Approvals returned inside the agreed SLA, most of the time |
| **Amber** | Latency has roughly doubled against their own first-quarter average |
| **Red** | Anything sitting unapproved for more than 14 days, or two consecutive requests unanswered |

Slowing approvals are the earliest behavioral signal of disengagement, and they usually appear a full quarter before anyone says anything. They also cause missed dates, which then get read as our failure, so this one compounds in both directions.

**What to do:** name it directly and without accusation. "Approvals have been running about two weeks lately, which is pushing the day 60 date. Is the approval sitting with the right person?" The answer is frequently that the person who approves has changed jobs, gone on leave, or lost interest, which is signal 6 wearing a disguise.

### 5. Meeting attendance and who attends

| | |
|---|---|
| **Green** | The named contact attends, and the economic buyer attends the QBR |
| **Amber** | Two consecutive monthly calls rescheduled, or the economic buyer skips a QBR |
| **Red** | A QBR declined outright, or attendance has been delegated down two levels |

Delegation downward is the most reliable pre-churn signal there is. Nobody cancels a vendor they are still meeting with personally.

**What to do:** get the economic buyer back in a room, with a reason that is worth their time. A hit guarantee, a strategic decision that genuinely needs them, or a result they will want to take credit for internally. Never ask for the meeting with "just to touch base."

### 6. Champion status

| | |
|---|---|
| **Green** | The internal champion is in place, engaged, and visibly using the work |
| **Amber** | The champion has changed role, gained new responsibilities, or gone quiet |
| **Red** | The champion has left, or a new decision-maker has arrived who did not choose us |

A new decision-maker who did not select Syntrex has no sunk cost in the decision and a natural incentive to review it. This is a common and entirely survivable churn cause, but only if you notice it in the month it happens.

**What to do:** re-onboard the new person as if they were a new client. Walk them through the kickoff summary, the baseline, the guaranteed metric, and every Receipt to date, in one meeting. The Receipts are the entire argument here, and this is the situation the immutability rule exists for: a stack of unedited monthly documents is a much stronger case than a conversation about how well things have gone.

### 7. Communication volume and tone

| | |
|---|---|
| **Green** | Regular two-way traffic. They send things unprompted |
| **Amber** | Traffic is one-directional, all from us, for more than 30 days |
| **Red** | Formal tone where it used to be informal, requests routed through a new person, or anyone on their side asking about the cancellation terms |

The tone shift is real and it is usually unconscious. When a client who wrote in fragments starts writing in full paragraphs with a subject line, something has changed on their side.

**What to do:** ask directly. "Is everything alright with how this is going? I would rather hear it early than at renewal." The answer to that question, asked at amber, is what determines whether you get the chance to fix it.

## Tier three: value and commercial signals

### 8. Value demonstrated versus value perceived

| | |
|---|---|
| **Green** | The client repeats our numbers back to us, or uses them internally |
| **Amber** | The client asks what they are getting for the money, in any phrasing |
| **Red** | The client disputes a number, or asks for a metric we do not report and cannot produce |

The question "remind me what we get for this" is never idle. It is a decision being formed out loud.

**What to do:** the answer to that question is a Receipt, not a conversation. Send the last three, plus a cumulative summary since engagement start. If they are asking for a metric we do not report and it is legitimate, add it to the Receipt going forward rather than arguing about whether it matters.

### 9. Usage and adoption of what we built

| | |
|---|---|
| **Green** | The things we built are being used: the assistant handles real volume, the automations run, the content gets shared internally |
| **Amber** | A significant deliverable has low or declining usage |
| **Red** | Something we built has been switched off, bypassed, or replaced |

A deliverable being bypassed is more dangerous than one that failed, because nobody reports it. They just stop using it, and it shows up as a value objection three months later.

**What to do:** find out why it was bypassed before proposing anything. Usually it is friction we could have removed, a workflow assumption that was wrong, or a person who was never trained on it. All three are cheap to fix and none of them get fixed if you do not ask.

### 10. Scope drift and unbilled creep

| | |
|---|---|
| **Green** | Requests are inside scope, or priced and approved when outside it |
| **Amber** | Regular small out-of-scope requests being absorbed without comment |
| **Red** | Significant unbilled work being delivered, or scope arguments occurring |

This is a margin signal that becomes a relationship signal. Absorbed creep breeds quiet resentment on our side and an inaccurate sense of what the engagement includes on theirs, and the correction, whenever it comes, feels to them like a new charge for something they were already getting.

**What to do:** price it. Not retroactively, which is a bad look and a worse conversation, but from the next request onward, referencing the change-handling section of the kickoff summary that they already agreed to.

### 11. Payment behavior

| | |
|---|---|
| **Green** | On time, no friction |
| **Amber** | Consistently late, or a payment method that keeps needing attention |
| **Red** | A payment missed without explanation, or an approval process newly introduced for an invoice that used to just get paid |

A new internal approval step appearing on a routine invoice means someone above your champion has started looking at the line item.

**What to do:** if it is process, fix the process. If it is cash flow, a payment plan is a better outcome for both sides than a cancellation, and offering it early is not a discount. Never respond to payment friction by discounting the rate.

### 12. Expansion trajectory

| | |
|---|---|
| **Green** | Expanded in the last two quarters, or an expansion is scheduled |
| **Amber** | Flat for three or more quarters with no expansion discussed |
| **Red** | Contracted, or a track was dropped |

Net revenue retention below 100% means the business leaks faster than it fills regardless of new logos. A flat account is not a stable account. It is an account where the relationship has stopped developing, and those churn.

**What to do:** the QBR is where this gets addressed, and only after a proven milestone. If the account has been flat for three quarters and the metric is being hit, the expansion conversation is overdue, not premature. If the metric is not being hit, the expansion conversation is not the problem to solve.

## Recovery play, for an At risk or Critical account

Run in this order. Do not skip to step 4, which is where the instinct goes.

1. **Diagnose before contacting.** Which of the twelve signals is red, and which of the three churn causes does it map to? Guidance, communication, or demonstrated value. The play is different for each and getting this wrong wastes the one conversation you get.
2. **Fix anything that is ours first.** A late Receipt, a missed call, a slipped date, a bypassed deliverable. Do not open a retention conversation while our own side of the cadence is broken, because they will hear the fix as a reaction to being caught.
3. **Get the economic buyer in a room,** with a reason worth their time.
4. **Lead with the evidence, not the relationship.** Every Receipt since engagement start, the guaranteed metric against baseline, cumulative hours returned, everything shipped. This is what the immutable Receipt archive is for. It is a much stronger argument than anything said in the meeting, because it was written before anyone was trying to save the account.
5. **Ask the direct question.** "What would make you cancel this?" It was question 11 on the intake checklist and it is worth asking again. People answer it honestly more often than you would expect.
6. **Propose a change, not a discount.** Price ranks sixth among reasons clients leave, so a discount solves the sixth problem while the first three remain. Never discount to save an account. Change the scope, change the metric if it is genuinely the wrong one, change the cadence, or change who does the reviewing.
7. **If they leave, exit cleanly.** Hand over everything they own, in a usable form, quickly and without friction. They own their deliverables and their data and that does not become negotiable at the exit. A clean exit is a future referral and sometimes a future client. A messy one travels further than five good outcomes in a referral business.
8. **Write down what actually happened.** Not the reason they gave. The signal that went red first, and how long it sat there before anyone acted. That is the only part of a churn that has any value.
